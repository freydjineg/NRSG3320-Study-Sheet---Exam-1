Nursing Care of Adults 1 · Modules 1–4

🔪 Module 1: Perioperative Care

Surgery is one of the highest-risk environments in nursing. This module covers everything from pre-op consent to post-op discharge.

💡

The BIG idea: The nurse is the patient's advocate at every phase — before, during, and after surgery.

⏰ The 3 Surgical Phases

Think of surgery like a road trip with three legs:

1
Pre-op (Before) — Packing your bags: consents, labs, NPO, teaching, IV access. Decision for surgery → patient rolls into OR.
2
Intra-op (During) — The drive: patient is in OR, under anesthesia, surgery happening. Ends when patient arrives in PACU.
3
Post-op (After) — Arriving home: PACU recovery, pain control, watching for complications. Ends when fully recovered.

⚠️ Surgery Urgency Types

TypeTime FrameExample
EmergentNOWRuptured aorta, stab wound, fractured skull
Urgent24–36 hrsInfected wound, closed fracture
RequiredWeeks–monthsThyroid surgery, cataracts, BPH
ElectiveWheneverHernia repair, cosmetic surgery

👵 Gerontologic Surgical Considerations

Older adults have reduced physiologic reserve — every system has less capacity to handle the stress of surgery and anesthesia. These changes make them higher risk for complications.

System-by-System Changes

  • Cardiac & Respiratory: Low reserves — poor tolerance of blood loss, fluid shifts, hypoxia. Slower recovery from anesthesia.
  • GI: Decreased motility — higher risk for paralytic ileus, constipation, aspiration.
  • Neurological: Post-op/ICU delirium is common. Baseline cognitive changes make assessment harder.
  • Renal & Hepatic: Depressed organ function — slower drug metabolism and excretion. Higher toxicity risk.
  • Integumentary: Decreased subcutaneous fat, fragile skin, more susceptible to pressure injuries and temperature changes.
💉 Drugs That Increase Surgical Risk
  • Anticoagulants (warfarin, heparin, NOACs) — increased bleeding risk. Must be held pre-op per protocol.
  • Corticosteroids — impair wound healing, mask infection signs, cause adrenal suppression.
  • NSAIDs — platelet dysfunction, GI bleeding risk. Hold 7-10 days pre-op.
  • Herbal supplements (ginkgo, garlic, fish oil, vitamin E) — antiplatelet effects. Hold 1-2 weeks pre-op.
  • Diuretics — electrolyte imbalances that affect anesthesia and cardiac stability.
  • Diabetic medications — hold metformin day of surgery; adjust insulin.

📋 Pre-Op: Informed Consent — Who Does What?

🧑‍⚕️ SurgeonExplains the procedure, risks, benefits, and alternatives. This is their legal responsibility.
🩺 NurseActs as a witness to the signature only. Makes sure the patient understood and wasn't coerced. Signs after the patient.
🚫 Do NOT Proceed If...Patient is confused, intoxicated, under 18 without guardian, or received sedation BEFORE signing.
🚨 Emergency ExceptionIf patient is unconscious and life is threatened → implied consent. Do not wait for signature. A competent patient who is awake can always refuse treatment.
📋 Pre-Op Checklist Essentials
  • Identification bracelet on patient
  • Allergy bracelet visible
  • Verify NPO status and IV access
  • Appropriate forms completed
  • Past medical history reviewed
  • Physical exam documented
  • Remove make-up, jewelry, dentures, glasses/contacts
  • Identify and mark surgical site
  • Administer pre-op medications (antibiotics must peak in bloodstream before incision)
  • Pre-op warming to prevent hypothermia

📚 Pre-Op Education — What to Teach
  • Pre-admission testing: Labs, health history, demographics, consent forms completed before admission day
  • What recovery looks like: devices, mobility, range-of-motion exercises, SCDs, IS
  • Pain management expectations — some pain is normal; multimodal approach including PCA
  • Cognitive coping strategies: relaxation techniques, distraction, guided imagery — goal is to reduce fear and anxiety

🛡️ Intra-Op: Surgical Team Roles

Circulating Nurse (Unsterile)Patient advocate. Verifies consent, calls Time Out, monitors aseptic technique, counts. Moves freely around the OR.
Scrub Nurse (Sterile)Handles instruments, maintains the sterile field, counts sponges/needles. Stays within the sterile zone only.
🦷 Full OR Team
  • Surgeon — performs the procedure
  • RNFA — Registered Nurse First Assist
  • CRNA / Anesthesiologist — manages anesthesia and airway throughout
  • Circulating Nurse — patient advocate, unsterile, roams freely
  • Scrub Nurse — sterile, handles instruments, performs counts (sponges, needles, instruments)
🧤 Sterile Field Details
  • Drapes: positioned front to back to maintain sterility
  • Items dropped into field from unsterile package
  • 1-foot perimeter around sterile field must be maintained
  • Breached = contaminated — remove immediately
  • Grounding equipment properly applied for electrosurgical safety
  • Latex allergy flagged — latex-free environment prepared

Sterile Field Rules — "When in Doubt, Throw It Out!"

  • Sterile gown = only front (chest to field level) and sleeves (2" above elbow to cuff)
  • Back of gown = NOT sterile
  • Anything below waist level = contaminated
  • Wet = contaminated (strike-through)
  • Never turn your back on the sterile field

🌐 WHO Surgical Safety Checklist

1
Sign In — Before anesthesia. Confirm identity, allergies, site marking, anesthesia check.
2
Time Out — Before skin incision. ENTIRE team stops. Confirm patient, procedure, site. Anyone can call a Time Out!
3
Sign Out — Before leaving OR. Count instruments, label specimens, document concerns.
🚨 The Time Out Prevents Wrong-Site SurgeryThis is the most important safety step. ALL work stops. Entire team confirms together.

🌡️ Intra-Op Complications — The Big 6

🔥 Malignant Hyperthermia (MH)Think: "The anesthesia drug lit a fire in the muscles." Triggered by volatile anesthetics (sevoflurane, halothane) & succinylcholine. Signs: temp >40°C, muscle rigidity, tachycardia, high CO2. Antidote: Dantrolene IV
🥵 HypothermiaOR is cold! Core temp <36.6°C. Risks: infection, slow healing, coagulopathy. Treat: warming blankets, warm IV fluids, cover patient.
🤮 Anesthesia-Related N/VRisk: female, <50 yrs, opioids. Turn to side + suction + antiemetics. Danger: aspiration pneumonia.
💥 AnaphylaxisMost common trigger: muscle relaxants (69%!), then latex (12%), then antibiotics. Treat: Epinephrine IM FIRST, always.
💭 Anesthesia AwarenessPatient is paralyzed but awake. Signs: rising BP, HR, movement. Monitor with EEG/brain waves.
💨 Respiratory ComplicationsInadequate ventilation, blocked airway, wrong tube placement (esophagus not trachea), hypoxia. Monitor with capnography (CO2 levels).

🏥 Post-Op: PACU Assessment — Think "HEAD TO TOE"

The PACU nurse checks everything systematically. Here's what matters most:

🪵 Airway & BreathingSpO2 >92%, RR 12-20. Remove oral airway when gag reflex returns. Reposition if snoring.
💉 CirculationBP, HR, warmth of skin. Bright red blood soaking dressing = emergency. Apply pressure, call provider NOW.
🧠 NeuroCan patient follow commands? Move extremities? What's their pain level?
💧 Urine OutputMinimum 30 mL/hour. Less = possible kidney compromise or shock.
🌡 TemperatureWarm up from OR hypothermia. Shivering increases O2 demand by 400%!
🤡 NauseaHave antiemetics ready. Side-lying position, suction available. Aspiration prevention!

🎯 PCA Pumps — Patient-Controlled Analgesia

A PCA pump lets the patient push a button to give themselves small doses of pain medication. The pump has a lockout period (usually 10 min) so they can't overdose themselves.

🚨 NEVER Do PCA by Proxy!Family members pressing the button while the patient sleeps = bypasses the safety mechanism. The patient's consciousness IS the safety check.
✅ Signs PCA is WorkingControlled pain, patient awake and comfortable, RR ≥12, SpO2 ≥92%.
🚨 Opioid Overdose SignsRR <12, SpO2 <90%, hard to wake up → Naloxone (Narcan) NOW
🎯 Multimodal Pain Management

The goal is to use multiple types of pain control together so opioid doses stay lower and side effects are reduced.

Pharmacological:

  • Opioids (PCA, oral)
  • NSAIDs (ketorolac)
  • Acetaminophen (scheduled)
  • Regional/epidural blocks

Non-pharmacological:

  • Relaxation techniques (deep breathing, progressive muscle relaxation)
  • Distraction (music, TV, conversation)
  • Guided imagery — visualizing peaceful scenes
  • Repositioning, ice/heat as appropriate
  • Splinting incision when coughing

📋 Post-Op Cardiovascular & Musculoskeletal Assessment CV: Monitor VS, watch for bleeding/hemorrhage/shock, cardiac dysrhythmias, thrombophlebitis. Encourage SCD use and early ambulation.
Musculoskeletal: Dangle at bedside BEFORE sitting up. Low-Fowler's unless contraindicated (increases thorax for lung expansion). q2h turning. Avoid supine until gag reflex returned. Ambulation progression starts POD 1.
🧠 Post-Op Neurological & Renal Assessment Neuro: Assess LOC — frequent periodic attempts to wake patient. Orient to environment. Speak in soft tone, filter noise. Maintain body temperature, monitor for hypothermia.
Renal: Monitor IV fluid administration, I&Os, urine output (min 30 mL/hr). Assess for bladder distension. Foley catheter removal as soon as appropriate to prevent CAUTI.
🚽 Urinary Retention Post-Op Common within 6–8 hours post-op. Causes: anesthesia, opioids, pain, anxiety, anti-cholinergic medications. Assessment: bladder scan, suprapubic fullness/discomfort, no voiding. Interventions: ambulate, run warm water, voiding position, privacy, straight cath if needed. Foley if persistent.

🩹 Wound Healing & Complications

Risk Factors for Poor Wound Healing

  • Older age, malnutrition, obesity
  • Poorly controlled diabetes
  • Tobacco use, poor oxygenation
  • Corticosteroids, anticoagulants
  • Wound stressors: vomiting, coughing, Valsalva
  • Hemorrhage/hypovolemia
🚨 Wound DehiscenceWound edges separate. More common in obese, malnourished, or steroid-treated patients. If bowel protrudes (evisceration): cover with sterile saline-soaked gauze, emergency surgery!
🧬 Types of Wound Drainage
  • Sanguineous = bright red blood (fresh, active)
  • Serosanguineous = pink/red tinged, watery (normal healing)
  • Serous = clear/yellow watery (normal)
  • Purulent = cloudy, yellow/green, smells = INFECTION
🩺 Wound Infection Signs (POD 3–7)Redness, warmth, swelling, purulent drainage, fever, elevated WBC. Treatment: wound culture, antibiotics, possible irrigation/debridement.

🚽 Post-Op GI Complications

🛑 Paralytic IleusBowel temporarily stops working from anesthesia or bowel manipulation. Signs: absent bowel sounds, no flatus, abdominal distention, vomiting. Treatment: NPO, NG tube, IV fluids, ambulation. Diet only resumes after bowel sounds return + flatus passes.
🚽 Urinary RetentionCommon within 8 hrs post-op. Cause: anesthesia, opioids, pain, anxiety. Assessment: bladder palpation/scan, patient discomfort. Intervention: ambulate, run warm water, straight cath if needed. Foley catheter if retention persists.

🚨 Thrombophlebitis & DVT Prevention

Inflammation of a vein with clot formation. Legs most commonly affected. Post-op patients are HIGH RISK (Virchow's Triad: stasis + hypercoagulability + endothelial injury).

Assessment

  • Vein feels hard, cord-like, tender
  • Swelling, warmth, redness of affected leg
  • Elevated temperature
  • Aching/cramping in calf

Prevention & Interventions

  • Anti-embolism stockings (remove twice daily to assess skin)
  • Sequential compression devices (SCDs) — on whenever in bed
  • Early ambulation — starting POD 1
  • Passive ROM every 2 hours
  • No dangling legs (increases venous stasis)
  • Anticoagulants as prescribed
  • Elevate 30 degrees with no pressure on popliteal area
🚨 Complication: Pulmonary Embolism (PE)Clot breaks off and travels to lungs. Signs: SUDDEN dyspnea, chest pain (pleuritic), tachycardia, hypoxia, anxiety, sense of doom. High-flow O2, rapid response team, prepare for anticoagulation.

📋 Discharge Planning & Teaching

✅ Discharge ReadinessPatient can walk, void, tolerate fluids, have a responsible adult caregiver and ride home. Cannot drive if on opioids or reflexes impaired.
🏠 What to TeachWound care, signs of infection (report to provider), medication instructions, activity restrictions (no lifting >10 lbs x4–6 wks for abdominal surgery), follow-up appointment, emergency contact info, when/where to seek care.

💧 Module 2: Fluids, Sodium & Potassium

Fluid and electrolyte balance is the foundation of every critically ill patient. Get this wrong and everything else falls apart.

💡

The BIG idea: Sodium controls where water goes. Potassium controls the heart. Know the normals, know the dangers.

🌊 Body Fluid Compartments — Where Is the Water?

Intracellular (ICF) — 40%Inside cells. 2/3 of all body fluid. K+ is the main electrolyte here.
Extracellular (ECF) — 20%Outside cells. Split between plasma (veins) and interstitial (between cells). Na+ is the main electrolyte here.
What moves water between them?Osmosis! Water moves toward higher concentration. Na+ controls ECF; K+ controls ICF.

📊 IV Fluid Types — Which Solution Does What?

Think of IV fluids like different strength lemonade. The "strength" is called osmolality/tonicity.

🧂 Sodium (Na+) — The Boss of Fluid Balance

Normal: 135–145 mEq/L
Think of sodium as a sponge — wherever sodium goes, water follows.

⬇️ Hyponatremia (Na+ <135)

Signs (brain swells): Headache, confusion, nausea, seizures, coma. Think: brain cells absorbing water = swelling = pressure.
  • Causes: Too much water (SIADH, beer potomania), vomiting, diuretics, heart/liver failure
  • SIADH: ADH won't turn off → kidneys keep holding water → blood gets diluted
  • Treatment: Mild = water restriction. Severe (seizures) = 3% NS (very slowly!). Max correction: 8–10 mEq/L per 24 hours or risk osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS) = brain stem damage!

⬆️ Hypernatremia (Na+ >145)

Signs (brain shrinks): Extreme thirst, agitation, confusion, seizures, coma. Brain cells losing water.
  • Causes: Diabetes insipidus (no ADH → massive urination), dehydration, too much salt
  • Treatment: Replace free water (hypotonic fluids or oral water) SLOWLY — same rule, correct slowly!

🧂 Sodium — Regulation & Hyponatremia Types

Sodium Regulation Na+ is the most abundant ECF electrolyte and the boss of fluid distribution. Regulated by three systems: (1) ADH (antidiuretic hormone) — released when osmolality rises; tells kidneys to retain water, diluting Na+. (2) Thirst mechanism — drives fluid intake when serum osmolality rises. (3) RAAS (renin-angiotensin-aldosterone) — aldosterone tells kidneys to retain Na+ (and water follows).

Hyponatremia (Na+ <135) — 3 Types by Volume Status

TypeVolume StatusCauses
EuvolemicNormal volume, diluted Na+SIADH, adrenal insufficiency, excess D5W or hypotonic tube feeds, head trauma
HypovolemicLow volume + low Na+Diuretics, GI losses (vomiting/diarrhea), excessive sweating
HypervolemicHigh volume, diluted Na+Heart failure, renal failure, cirrhosis
Hyponatremia SignsSeizures, stupor, lethargy, confusion. Abdominal cramping, poor appetite, overactive bowel sounds. Muscle spasms, diminished reflexes. Decreased UO. Orthostatic hypotension. Shallow respirations.
Hypernatremia SignsFatigue, restlessness, muscle twitching, seizures (severe). Increased fluid retention, edema, decreased UO. Extreme thirst, dry mouth. CNS changes (brain cells shrink). Treat: gradual Na+ lowering with isotonic or 0.45% NS, restrict sodium.

⚡ Potassium (K+) — The Heart Electrolyte

Normal: 3.5–5.0 mEq/L
K+ controls heart rhythm and muscle contraction. Small changes = BIG consequences.

⬇️ Hypokalemia (K+ <3.5)

Signs: Muscle weakness, cramps, flat T-waves & U-waves on ECG, constipation, shallow breathing if severe.
  • Causes: Diuretics (furosemide, HCTZ), vomiting, NG suction, diarrhea, steroids
  • Danger: Hypokalemia + digoxin = LETHAL toxicity risk
  • Treatment: KCl replacement. IV potassium: NEVER IV push. Max 10 mEq/hr peripheral. Always on a pump with cardiac monitoring.

⬆️ Hyperkalemia (K+ >5.0)

Signs: Peaked T-waves, wide QRS, V-fib, muscle weakness. K+ >7.0 = cardiac arrest risk!
💊 Newer K+ Binders
  • Lokelma (Sodium Zirconium Cyclosilicate) — newer, preferred potassium binder. Selectively traps K+ in GI tract, onset 1-2 hours, no sorbitol needed, better tolerated than Kayexalate
  • Kayexalate (Sodium Polystyrene Sulfonate) — older binder, slower (hours to days), requires sorbitol (risk of GI necrosis)
  • Both remove K+ from the body — unlike insulin/bicarb which only shift it temporarily
  • Causes: Kidney failure, ACE inhibitors, spironolactone, cell death (burns, rhabdo)
  • Treatment sequence: C-BIG-K: Calcium gluconate → Bicarb → Insulin+Glucose → Kayexalate → (dialysis)

📉 Fluid Volume Deficit — 3 Types, Causes & Manifestations

TypeWhat's LostWhat HappensFluid Replacement
Isotonic (Hypovolemia) Water + electrolytes lost equally Decreased circulating blood volume + inadequate tissue perfusion. No cell shrinkage/swelling. Isotonic fluids (0.9% NS, LR)
Hypertonic dehydration Water loss > electrolyte loss Plasma electrolytes rise. Fluid shifts from ICF to plasma → cells shrink. Very concentrated blood. Hypotonic fluids (0.45% NS)
Hypotonic dehydration Electrolyte loss > water loss Plasma osmolality drops. Fluid shifts from plasma into cells → cells swell. Decreased plasma volume. Hypertonic fluids (3% NS)

Common Causes of Fluid Deficit

  • Vomiting, diarrhea, NG suctioning
  • Excessive diuresis or sweating
  • Hemorrhage, burns, wound drainage
  • Inadequate oral intake (NPO, dysphagia)
  • Third spacing (peritonitis, bowel obstruction)

Clinical Manifestations

  • Tachycardia, weak thready pulse
  • Hypotension, orthostatic hypotension
  • Dry mucous membranes, decreased skin turgor
  • Dark concentrated urine, decreased UO (<30 mL/hr)
  • Flat neck veins, sunken eyes
  • Confusion, lethargy (severe)

🩸 Blood Transfusions — Safety First

Before You Start

  • Two-nurse verification at bedside (patient name, DOB, blood type, unit number)
  • Prime with 0.9% NS only (LR causes clotting, dextrose causes hemolysis)
  • Complete within 4 hours of leaving blood bank

Vital Signs Schedule

  • Before starting
  • 15 minutes after start (STAY at bedside — most reactions start here)
  • Every 30 minutes during
  • After completion
🚨 Acute Hemolytic Reaction — Most Dangerous!ABO mismatch. Signs: fever, chills, LOWER BACK PAIN, dark urine (hemoglobinuria). STOP transfusion immediately! Keep IV open with NS. Call provider + blood bank.
Red Man Syndrome (Vancomycin)Not a true allergy! Rate-related flushing, itching, red rash over face/neck. Slow infusion + diphenhydramine. Can continue if tolerated.

📈 Fluid Volume Overload — Causes & Manifestations

Common Causes

  • Excessive IV fluid administration (too fast or too much)
  • Heart failure (pump failure → fluid backup)
  • Renal failure (cannot excrete fluid)
  • Cirrhosis (low albumin → fluid leaks out of vessels)
  • Excess sodium intake
  • Corticosteroids (sodium/fluid retention)
  • Cushing's syndrome (aldosterone excess)

Clinical Manifestations

  • Bounding pulse, hypertension
  • JVD (jugular venous distension)
  • Crackles/rales in lung bases (pulmonary edema)
  • Peripheral pitting edema (ankles, sacrum)
  • Ascites, weight gain
  • Dyspnea, orthopnea
  • S3 heart sound (ventricular gallop)
💉 Management Restrict fluid + sodium intake. Diuretics: mild = HCTZ, severe = furosemide, K+-sparing = spironolactone. Monitor I&Os and daily weight. Prepare for dialysis if refractory.

🌊 Second & Third Spacing — Where Does the Fluid Go?

Second Spacing (Edema)Fluid leaks from capillaries into the interstitial tissue space. This is normal edema. Causes: increased hydrostatic pressure (CHF), decreased oncotic pressure (low albumin), inflammation (burns, trauma).
Third Spacing 🚨Fluid gets trapped in a body space where it can't be used — like the peritoneal cavity (ascites), pleural space, or pericardial space. It's a functional loss — the fluid exists but the body cannot access it for circulation. Signs of hypovolemia despite visible fluid!
💡 Oncotic (Colloid Osmotic) PressureThis is the "suction force" that keeps fluid inside blood vessels. Albumin is the main protein creating this. If albumin drops (cirrhosis, malnutrition, nephrotic syndrome) → oncotic pressure falls → fluid leaks into tissues → edema despite being hypovolemic! Give albumin to restore this.

⚡ Potassium ECG Changes — Know Them Cold!

K+ LevelECG Changes (in order of severity)Clinical Meaning
HypoKalemia (<3.5)Flat/inverted T-waves → Prominent U-wave (after T-wave) → ST depression → Widened QRS (severe)U-wave is the CLASSIC sign. Easy to miss — looks like an extra bump after T.
HyperKalemia (>5.0)Peaked (tent-shaped) T-waves → Flat P-waves → Widened QRS → Sine wave → V-Fib/AsystolePeaked T-waves are the FIRST sign. K+ >6.5 = cardiac emergency. K+ >7.0 = imminent arrest.
🚨 IV Potassium Safety Rules
  • K+ 2.5–3.5: ORAL supplementation preferred
  • K+ <2.5: IV supplementation required
  • NEVER IV push (causes immediate cardiac arrest)
  • Max peripheral rate: 10 mEq/hr
  • Always on infusion pump with cardiac monitoring
  • Dilute in minimum 100 mL NS

📊 Plasma Expanders (Colloids)

Colloids stay in the vascular system because the large molecules can't cross capillary walls. Think of them as a plug for the blood vessels — they fill up the tank and stay there.

AlbuminNatural protein. Rapidly expands plasma volume. Used in shock, severe hypoalbuminemia, and when crystalloids alone are insufficient.
Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP)Contains all clotting factors. Used for coagulopathy, warfarin reversal, DIC. Requires ABO compatibility.
PRBCs (Packed Red Cells)Restore oxygen-carrying capacity. Use when Hgb <7-8 g/dL (symptomatic anemia) or acute blood loss. Must check compatibility!

🧠 Module 3: Neurological Disorders

Brain and nerve emergencies move fast. Every minute of delay costs neurons. This module covers strokes, seizures, degenerative diseases, and ICP.

💡

The BIG idea: "Time is brain." Recognize deficits fast, protect the airway, and never treat the wrong stroke type.

🧠 Stroke (CVA) — "A Brain Attack"

A stroke is like a heart attack — but in the brain. Either a blocked blood vessel (ischemic) or a burst blood vessel (hemorrhagic) cuts off oxygen to brain cells.

🚧 Ischemic Stroke (87% of strokes)Blocked pipe. Either a traveling clot (embolic) or a clot that formed in place (thrombotic). A-fib is the #1 cause of embolic stroke.
Can treat with tPA if caught in time!
💥 Hemorrhagic Stroke (13%)Burst pipe. Blood floods the brain, raises pressure. #1 risk factor: HYPERTENSION.
tPA is CONTRAINDICATED! Would worsen bleeding.
⚠️ TIA — The Warning ShotTemporary stroke symptoms that resolve in minutes to hours. Brain's 911 call. Up to 15% of TIA patients have a full stroke within 3 months — often within 48 hours. URGENT evaluation required!
📋 CT Scan First — Always!Before ANY stroke treatment, get a non-contrast CT to rule out bleeding. Treating hemorrhagic stroke with tPA = fatal error.

Right vs. Left Brain Stroke — High Yield!

⚠️ TIA, Stroke Risk Factors & Right vs Left Brain

⚠️ TIA — Transient Ischemic Attack Temporary neurologic deficit from temporary impairment of blood flow. Symptoms RESOLVE completely within 24 hours (usually minutes). This is a WARNING SIGN — 10% of TIA patients have a stroke within 90 days. Requires urgent assessment. Treat like a stroke until proven otherwise.

Stroke Risk Factors

  • Modifiable: HTN (#1 risk factor), diabetes, hyperlipidemia, atrial fibrillation, smoking, obesity, physical inactivity, alcohol use, oral contraceptives
  • Non-modifiable: Age, male sex, African American race, family history, prior TIA or stroke
  • AFib: left atrial clot → embolism = 5× stroke risk
Stroke Diagnostic Tests
  • CT head without contrast (FIRST — rules out hemorrhage)
  • MRI (better for ischemic areas, not first-line)
  • 12-lead ECG (detect AFib)
  • Carotid Doppler ultrasound
  • CBC, coagulation studies, glucose
  • Echocardiogram (detect cardiac source)

⏱️ tPA (Alteplase) — The Clot Buster

✅ Give tPA When...Ischemic stroke confirmed (no blood on CT), within 3–4.5 hours of last known well, no contraindications.
🚨 Hold tPA When...BP >185/110 (treat BP first), recent surgery, bleeding disorder, hemorrhagic stroke.
🚨 After tPA — 24-Hour BlackoutNO aspirin, NO heparin, NO anticoagulants, NO Foley/NG unless critical, NO arterial lines. Watch for hemorrhagic conversion: sudden headache, neuro worsening → STOP tPA if still running.
📉 BP RuleIschemic stroke: allow BP up to 220/120 (permissive hypertension → feeds ischemic tissue). Hemorrhagic stroke: target SBP <140–160 (prevent more bleeding).

🧠 Stroke Assessment Findings, Prevention & Carotid Endarterectomy

Common Assessment Findings

  • Agnosia (right brain): inability to recognize familiar objects or people
  • Apraxia/dyspraxia (left brain): cannot carry out purposeful movements despite physical ability
  • Hemianopsia (both): blindness in half the visual field — scan room, dress weak side first
  • Neglect syndrome (right brain): patient unaware their paralyzed side exists — touch both sides
  • Proprioception alterations (right/both): altered position sense → fall risk
  • Aphasia (left brain): expressive (knows what to say, can't say it) vs receptive (can speak but can't understand)
Stroke Prevention Medications
  • Antiplatelets (aspirin, clopidogrel): for ischemic stroke prevention in patients with TIA or prior stroke
  • Anticoagulants (warfarin, NOACs): for AFib-related stroke prevention
  • Statins: reduce atherosclerosis progression
  • Antihypertensives: controlling HTN is the #1 modifiable risk
Carotid Endarterectomy Surgical removal of atherosclerotic plaque from the carotid artery to reduce stroke risk. Used when carotid stenosis is significant. Can be done under general or local/regional anesthesia. Post-op: monitor for stroke symptoms, cranial nerve deficits, hematoma at incision site.
♿ Chronic Phase — Independence with Assistive DevicesPromote independence using assistive devices for ADLs (cane, walker, grab bars, splints, adapted utensils). PT/OT referral and involvement. Encourage fluids and high-fiber diet with stool softeners. Unilateral neglect: touch and use both sides. Hemianopsia: scan full field, approach from unaffected side, dress weak side first.

⚡ Seizures — Electrical Storms in the Brain

A seizure is like a sudden power surge — brain cells fire uncontrollably. Epilepsy is when this happens chronically.

During a Seizure — Priority Actions

1
Note the time! Status epilepticus = >5 minutes. Call for help.
2
Side-lying position to protect airway from secretions.
3
Do NOT restrain limbs (fracture risk) or put anything in the mouth!
4
IV Lorazepam (Ativan) is first-line for status epilepticus (>5 min).
5
Check blood glucose — hypoglycemia causes seizures!
Postictal PhaseNormal exhausted/confused state after seizure. Can include Todd's paralysis (temporary weakness) that resolves in hours. Don't panic — document and monitor.
🚨 Phenytoin (Dilantin) IV RulesMax rate: 50 mg/min. Mix in NS only (precipitates in dextrose!). Therapeutic: 10–20 mcg/mL. Monitor BP + cardiac rhythm.
Safety TeachingNo driving until seizure-free (6–12 mo + MD clearance). No swimming alone. Never stop medication without provider.

⚡ Seizure Types — Complete Classification

TypeCharacteristicsKey Facts
Tonic-clonic (Grand mal) Tonic phase: stiffening 10-20 sec → loss of consciousness. Clonic phase: rapid jerking 30 sec. Bladder/bowel may release. Full recovery takes hours. >5 min = status epilepticus = emergency.
Absence (Petit mal) Brief seconds, daydream appearance, no loss of muscle tone. May or may not lose consciousness. Common in children. Up to 100x/day untreated. Immediate recovery.
Myoclonic Sudden brief jerking of one or more muscles. May affect arms, legs, trunk. Brief, sudden. Can be normal (hypnic jerks when falling asleep) or pathologic.
Atonic (Drop attack) Sudden loss of muscle tone → patient drops to ground. No warning. Injury risk is very high — helmet may be needed. Immediate recovery of consciousness.
Simple partial (focal) Motor, sensory, or autonomic symptoms in ONE part of body. Consciousness PRESERVED. Stays localized. Patient aware throughout. May spread (Jacksonian march).
Complex partial Impaired consciousness + automatisms (lip smacking, picking at clothes). Temporal lobe origin. Post-ictal confusion. Patient not aware of actions during seizure.
📚 Post-Seizure Patient Education
  • Never stop AEDs without provider guidance — can trigger status epilepticus
  • No driving until seizure-free per state law (typically 6-12 months) + provider clearance
  • No swimming or bathing alone, no heights, no heavy machinery
  • Identify and avoid triggers (sleep deprivation, alcohol, stress, flashing lights)
  • Wear medical alert bracelet. Carry emergency medication if prescribed.
  • Check blood levels regularly if on phenytoin (Dilantin) — narrow therapeutic index

🦠 Parkinson's Disease — Dopamine Slowly Running Out

Think of Parkinson's as a car engine that's slowly running out of oil. The brain loses the cells that make dopamine — the chemical that smooths out movement. Without it, movements become slow, stiff, and shaky.

The 4 Cardinal Signs — TRAP

  • Tremor at rest (pill-rolling)
  • Rigidity (cogwheel, lead-pipe)
  • Akinesia/bradykinesia (slow movement, shuffling gait)
  • Postural instability (fall risk!)
🚨 NEVER Give These to Parkinson's Patients:Haloperidol (Haldol), metoclopramide (Reglan), prochlorperazine. All block dopamine and will cause ACUTE WORSENING of symptoms!
💊 Sinemet (Carbidopa-Levodopa) TeachingTake 30–60 min BEFORE meals. High-protein meals compete with absorption. NEVER stop abruptly (NMS-like crisis). Dark urine/sweat is harmless. Rise slowly (orthostatic hypotension).
On-Off PhenomenonUnpredictable fluctuations between "on" (good control) and "off" (severe stiffness). Related to medication timing. More frequent smaller doses or a COMT inhibitor may help.

🧬 Multiple Sclerosis — The Fraying Wires

MS is like having the insulation stripped off electrical wires. The immune system attacks the myelin sheath (protective coating on nerves), causing messages to misfire or not get through at all.

Common Symptoms

  • Visual changes (optic neuritis) — blurry/double vision
  • Weakness, spasticity, coordination problems
  • Fatigue (the #1 complaint)
  • Bladder dysfunction
  • Sensory changes (numbness, tingling)
Uhthoff's PhenomenonHeat makes symptoms temporarily WORSE (demyelinated nerves can't conduct in heat). Resolves with cooling. NOT a relapse. Teach: cool showers, cooling vest, avoid hot tubs.
🚨 MS RelapseNew or worsening symptoms lasting >24 hours = relapse. Treat with IV methylprednisolone (Solu-Medrol). Speeds recovery but doesn't change long-term outcome.
💹 Natalizumab (Tysabri) Warning!Highly effective disease-modifying therapy but risk of PML (progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy) — a potentially fatal brain infection from JC virus reactivation. Enrolled in TOUCH monitoring program.

🧬 MS — Assessment Findings in Detail

Common MS Assessment Findings

  • Vision: optic neuritis (blurred/double vision), nystagmus — eye patch for diplopia
  • Motor: weakness, spasticity, ataxia (coordination), tremor
  • Fatigue: most common and disabling symptom — worsens with heat (Uhthoff's)
  • Bladder: urgency, frequency, retention, incontinence — toileting schedule
  • Bowel: constipation (common), incontinence
  • Cognitive: memory problems, slowed processing, depression
  • Speech: dysarthria, scanning speech
  • Sensory: numbness, tingling, L'Hermitte's sign (electric shock down spine with neck flexion)
Secondary Headaches Headaches caused by an underlying condition — not a primary headache disorder. Causes include: head trauma, infections (meningitis), medication overuse ("rebound headache"), hypertensive crisis, intracranial masses, subarachnoid hemorrhage (thunderclap — "worst headache of life" = emergency!). Must rule out dangerous causes before treating symptomatically.
MS Triggers (Exacerbation) Pregnancy, trauma, infection, fatigue, stress, heat. Uhthoff's phenomenon = temporary worsening from heat — NOT a relapse. Cool environment, avoid hot showers.

💪 ALS — "Lou Gehrig's Disease"

ALS is like a power outage that slowly spreads through the building. Motor neurons die, so the brain can no longer send movement signals — but cognitive function stays intact in most patients. The patient is fully aware of everything happening to them.

📋 Key FeaturesProgressive motor weakness and paralysis. Cognition spared. Eventual respiratory failure is the cause of death. No cure — management focuses on quality of life.
🚨 Nursing PriorityDysphagia → aspiration pneumonia (leading cause of death). PEG tube recommended BEFORE FVC drops below 50%. Safety: thickened liquids, chin tuck, SLP evaluation.

📈 ICP — Increased Intracranial Pressure

The skull is a rigid box. If anything inside swells (blood, fluid, tumor) — pressure builds and crushes brain tissue. Think of it like squeezing a water balloon inside a box.

Cushing's Triad = LATE/Severe ICP

  • Hypertension with widened pulse pressure
  • Bradycardia (slow heart rate)
  • Irregular respirations

🚨 Cushing's Triad = brain herniation is imminent. Emergency!

ICP Nursing Priorities

1
HOB at 30 degrees — promotes venous drainage
2
Head in midline — no neck turning (kinks jugular veins)
3
Avoid Valsalva — no straining, coughing, hip flexion >90°
4
Mannitol IV = osmotic diuretic — pulls fluid out of brain

🤕 Headaches — Primary Types

Tension Headache (most common)Dull, band-like pressure around head. Cause: stress, poor posture, muscle tension, lack of sleep, prolonged screen time. Treatment: OTC analgesics, stress reduction, relaxation techniques.
Cluster Headache (rare, excruciating)Intense, localized around ONE eye. Comes in clusters (same time daily for weeks). Runs and eye watering on affected side. Treatment: high-flow O2 therapy for 15–20 minutes, triptans. Emergency if first severe headache — rule out subarachnoid hemorrhage!
Migraine (recurrent, severe)Often unilateral, throbbing. Accompanied by nausea, photophobia (light sensitivity), phonophobia (sound sensitivity). Can have aura (visual disturbances, numbness before headache). Phases: prodromal → aura → headache → postdrome.

Migraine Phases

1
Prodromal (hours–days before): Mood changes, food cravings, fatigue, yawning, neck stiffness. The body signals a migraine is coming.
2
Aura (not all patients): Neurological symptoms 20–60 min before headache. Visual zigzag lines, blind spots, tingling, difficulty speaking. Fully reversible.
3
Headache phase (4–72 hrs): Throbbing, usually one-sided. Nausea, vomiting, extreme sensitivity to light/sound. Even walking worsens pain.
4
Postdrome ("migraine hangover"): Exhaustion, brain fog, neck stiffness. Can last 24–48 hours.
💉 Migraine Nursing InterventionsDark, quiet room. Cold compress. Antiemetics. Triptans (sumatriptan) = first-line acute treatment. Teach patient to identify and avoid personal triggers (sleep disruption, hormonal changes, certain foods, stress).

🧠 MS — More Detail from Slides

💪 Parkinson's — Nutrition & Movement Tips from Slides

Diet & FluidsHigh-protein, high-calorie, high-fiber soft diet. Small, frequent feedings. Increase fluid intake to 2 L/day. Monitor for constipation. Check swallowing ability before every meal.
Mobility Strategies (Lecture Pearl)Rock back and forth to initiate movement. Use visual cues (lines on floor) to trigger walking. Allow extra time — never rush. Avoid prolonged sitting. Proper posture (reduce kyphosis).
💊 Antiparkinsonian Drug ClassesDopaminergics (Sinemet/carbidopa-levodopa): restore dopamine. Contraindicated in cardiac, renal, psychiatric disorders.

❤️ Module 4: Cardiovascular Disorders

Cardiovascular disease is the #1 killer worldwide. This module covers hypertension, heart failure, CAD, MI, and vascular disorders.

💡

The BIG idea: Know your left from your right (heart failure), stable from unstable (angina), arterial from venous (ulcers).

📊 Hypertension — The Silent Killer

High blood pressure usually has NO symptoms until organs are already damaged. By the time you feel it, something's already breaking.

🩹 Hypokalemia ECG Changes
  • Flat or inverted T-waves
  • Prominent U-wave (bump after T-wave)
  • ST depression

⚡ Hyperkalemia Management
  • Calcium gluconate IV — stabilizes heart (does not lower K+)
  • Insulin + Dextrose — shifts K+ into cells (temporary)
  • Sodium bicarb — shifts K+ intracellularly
  • Kayexalate / Lokelma — removes K+ from body in stool
  • Dialysis — for critically high levels
  • ECG: peaked T → wide QRS → flat P → sine wave = emergency

Non-Drug Treatment — DASH

Sodium restriction ≤2g/day, weight loss, exercise, quit smoking, reduce alcohol and caffeine, stress management. Try lifestyle for 1–3 months before adding medication for Stage 1.

📊 Hypertension — Types, Assessment & End-Organ Damage

Types of Hypertension

  • Primary (essential): No identifiable cause. 90-95% of cases. Multifactorial (genetics, lifestyle, age).
  • Secondary: Caused by an identifiable condition — renal disease, hyperaldosteronism, sleep apnea, thyroid disease, medications.
  • Malignant: Severely elevated, rapidly progressive. Medical emergency with organ damage.
  • Isolated systolic HTN: Common in older adults — vessel stiffness → SBP rises but DBP normal/low. Difficult to treat.

Assessment

  • Usually asymptomatic (“silent killer”)
  • Headache (occipital, AM) — classic but uncommon
  • Epistaxis (nosebleed), dizziness, visual changes
  • Check BP in BOTH arms; use correct cuff size
  • Orthostatic measurements in elderly

End-Organ Damage

Target Organ Damage from Uncontrolled HTN
  • Heart: LV hypertrophy → HF, CAD, MI, dysrhythmias
  • Brain: Stroke, TIA, hypertensive encephalopathy, vascular dementia
  • Kidneys: CKD, proteinuria, renal failure
  • Eyes: Hypertensive retinopathy, papilledema, vision loss
  • Peripheral vessels: PAD, aortic aneurysm
LV Hypertrophy The heart compensates for high afterload by building bigger muscle. Short term: maintains output. Long term: stiff ventricle (diastolic dysfunction), impaired filling, HF, arrhythmias. Detected on ECG and echo.
HTN Pharmacological Classes Diuretics (thiazides HCTZ — first line), ACE inhibitors, ARBs, beta-blockers, CCBs. Combination therapy often required. Goal: reduce end-organ damage, not just lower numbers.

💔 Heart Failure — When the Pump Gives Out

Left-Sided HF — Lungs FillLeft ventricle can't pump out → blood backs up into lungs. Signs: dyspnea, orthopnea, PND (waking up gasping at night), crackles, pink frothy sputum, S3 gallop.
Right-Sided HF — Body FillsRight ventricle fails → blood backs up into the body. Signs: JVD, peripheral edema, ascites, hepatomegaly. Most common cause: left-sided HF!
🎯 HFrEF vs. HFpEFHFrEF (EF ≤40%): Systolic failure. Heart squeezes poorly. Treat with ACE-I, beta-blocker, aldosterone antagonist, SGLT2 inhibitor.

HFpEF (EF ≥50%): Diastolic failure. Heart is stiff, won't fill. Treat symptoms, control BP.
🚨 Call Provider If...Weight gain >2–3 lbs/24hr or >5 lbs/week, worsening shortness of breath, new S3 gallop, pink frothy sputum, JVD, altered mental status.

Digoxin — The Dangerous Helper

What it DoesIncreases heart contraction strength (positive inotrope). Slows heart rate. Used for HF + A-fib.
🚨 Toxicity Watch!Therapeutic: 0.5–2 ng/mL. Toxic: >2 ng/mL. Signs: anorexia, N/V, yellow-green halos, bradycardia. HOLD if HR <60 bpm. Hypokalemia DOUBLES toxicity risk!
📋 Diagnosing Heart Failure
  • Echocardiogram — EF <40% = HFrEF; EF ≥50% = HFpEF; shows ventricle size
  • BNP / NT-proBNP — elevated = ventricular wall stress. Key lab for HF
  • Chest X-ray — cardiomegaly, pulmonary edema, pleural effusion
  • EKG — dysrhythmias, LVH pattern
  • Labs: cardiac enzymes (rule out MI), CMP (renal function)
  • Stress test, cardiac catheterization if needed
💚 Low Cardiac Output / Poor Perfusion Signs
  • Fatigue and weakness (muscles not perfused)
  • Cool, pale extremities (peripheral vasoconstriction)
  • Decreased urine output <30 mL/hr (kidneys not perfused)
  • Confusion / restlessness (brain not perfused)
  • Hypotension, tachycardia (compensatory)
  • Narrow pulse pressure
⚖️ HF Self-Monitoring: Daily Weight + Fluid Restriction
  • Daily weight: same time, same scale, same clothes each morning. Report >2-3 lbs in 24 hours OR >5 lbs in one week to provider
  • Fluid restriction if prescribed: typically 1.5-2 L/day in advanced HF
  • Low-sodium diet ≤2g/day. Avoid large amounts of caffeine
  • Balance rest and activity periods. Avoid isometric exercises
🧓 Gerontologic HF: Prostate + Diuretic ResistanceElderly men on diuretics: monitor for bladder distention from urethral obstruction (enlarged prostate). Diuretics increase urine output and can precipitate acute urinary retention. Monitor voiding, check for distension, Foley if retention. Older adults may be resistant to diuretics (decreased renal function) and more sensitive to volume changes.

🩼 Coronary Artery Disease & MI

Coronary artery disease is like rust buildup inside a water pipe (atherosclerosis). When the pipe gets completely blocked, the part of the heart it feeds dies — that's a heart attack (MI).

🩼 CAD — Risk Factors, Diagnostics, Angina Interventions & Older Adults

CAD Risk Factors

  • Modifiable: HTN, smoking, hyperlipidemia (high LDL, low HDL), diabetes, obesity, physical inactivity, stress, alcohol
  • Non-modifiable: Age (>45 men, >55 women), male sex, family history, prior CAD
  • Atherosclerosis symptoms: left main >50% reduction OR any vessel >75% = ischemia

CAD Clinical Manifestations

  • Classic: pressure/tightness/crushing chest pain, radiates to left arm, jaw, shoulder, back
  • Diaphoresis, nausea, dyspnea
  • ECG: ST depression = ischemia; ST elevation = infarction

Diagnostic Testing

  • ECG: ST depression (ischemia) vs elevation (infarction)
  • Lipid profile: LDL vs HDL ratio
  • Stress test: exercise or pharmacologic
  • Cardiac catheterization: definitive, shows lesion location and severity
Angina Interventions
  • Nitroglycerin SL (stable angina): 1 tab q5min x3, call 911 if unrelieved
  • Rest and activity modification
  • O2 as needed
  • Beta-blockers: reduce O2 demand (decrease HR and contractility)
  • Long-acting nitrates for prevention
  • CCBs for variant (Prinzmetal) angina
  • Aspirin/antiplatelet therapy
  • Statins: stabilize plaque, reduce progression
  • PCI or CABG for significant stenosis
🧓 Older Adult CAD Considerations Diminished pain perception with aging → “silent CAD.” May present with weakness, indigestion, fatigue instead of chest pain. Teach patients to recognize their own atypical “chest-pain equivalent.” Pharmacologic stress testing for surgical clearance. Medications need dose adjustments; used cautiously.

🦵 Peripheral Vascular Disease

PAD — Peripheral Arterial DiseaseNot enough blood getting to legs. Claudication (leg pain with walking, relieved by rest). Signs: pale, cool, no pulse, shiny hairless skin, ulcers on toes/pressure points. ABI <0.9 = PAD. ABI >1.3 = calcified vessels (diabetics) → use toe-brachial index instead.
Treatment for PADStop smoking (#1!), exercise (builds collateral vessels), antiplatelet + statin, endovascular angioplasty, surgical bypass. Last resort: amputation.
Venous InsufficiencyBlood pooling in legs. Medial ankle ulcers, varicose veins, brown/leathery skin (hemosiderin), pitting edema. Signs: warm, brown, ulcer at medial malleolus.
🧦 Compression TherapyMust check ABI ≥0.8 before applying compression (arterial disease = compression causes ischemia!). Apply in morning before standing. Graduated compression.
🧓 Vascular Gerontologic Changes
  • Aging causes vessel wall changes — walls stiffen and lose elasticity
  • Stiff vessels = increased peripheral resistance
  • Impaired blood flow and O2/nutrient delivery to tissues
  • Increased left ventricular workload (heart works harder against stiff vessels)
  • Explains why isolated systolic HTN is common in elderly
💊 PAD Treatment — Medications
  • Antiplatelet therapy: Aspirin, Clopidogrel — prevent clot formation
  • Cilostazol (Pletal): PDE III inhibitor — vasodilation + antiplatelet → improves claudication walking distance. CONTRAINDICATED in HF
  • High-dose statins: Stabilize plaque, reduce LDL
  • Endovascular: angioplasty ± stent, atherectomy
  • Surgical: bypass grafts

💓 Coronary Artery Disease (CAD) & Angina

CAD is the slow buildup of fatty plaque (atherosclerosis) inside coronary arteries. When blood flow is reduced enough — symptoms appear. Ischemia occurs when any vessel is reduced by 75%.

3 Types of Angina

Stable AnginaPredictable chest pain triggered by exertion or stress. Relieved in <15 minutes with rest or nitroglycerin. Same pattern each time. The plaque is stable.
Unstable Angina (Pre-infarction) 🚨New onset, occurs at rest, or worsening pattern. NOT relieved by nitroglycerin. Indicates plaque rupture. Treat as ACS — hospital admission, cardiac monitoring, anticoagulation.
Variant/Prinzmetal AnginaCaused by coronary artery SPASM, not plaque. Occurs at rest, often at same time each day. Treat with calcium channel blockers and nitrates.

CAD Clinical Manifestations

  • Classic chest pain: pressure, tightness, "elephant on chest"
  • Radiates to left arm, jaw, shoulder, back
  • Diaphoresis, nausea, dyspnea
  • Older adults may have "silent" CAD — fatigue, weakness, indigestion instead of chest pain!
CAD PreventionTobacco cessation, manage HTN and DM, low-sodium/low-cholesterol diet, exercise, stress reduction, reduce alcohol, statins.

🥶 Raynaud's Phenomenon

Vasospasm of small arteries in response to cold or stress. Think of it like the blood vessels in your fingers panicking and slamming shut when they're cold.

Clinical Signs — Color Change Sequence

  • White (pallor) — vasospasm cuts off blood
  • Blue (cyanosis) — oxygen depletes
  • Red (rubor) — blood rushes back when vessel opens

Plus: numbness, tingling, swelling and cold at affected site. Affects fingers, toes, ears, cheeks.

Nursing Interventions

  • Monitor pulses and skin integrity
  • Administer vasodilators and CCBs as ordered
  • Identify and AVOID triggers (cold, stress, caffeine, tobacco)
  • Warm clothing, gloves, socks at all times
  • Use warm water during an attack — NOT hot! (can burn numb tissue)
  • Smoking cessation (nicotine causes vasoconstriction)
  • Protect fingers/hands from injury

🦵 Lymphedema

Obstruction of lymphatic vessels causes lymph fluid to pool in tissues. Marked in dependent positions (arms, legs). Can be primary (congenital) or secondary (after lymph node removal, e.g., post-mastectomy).

SignsPitting edema worse with dependency, skin thickening over time, feeling of heaviness, reduced mobility. Affected limb is at high infection risk.
TreatmentAmbulatory-compression stockings, Manual Lymphatic Drainage (MLD) massage, physiotherapy, skin care (moisturize, avoid injury/infection). Patients must protect the affected limb lifelong.

🦵 PAD Nursing Interventions — From the Slides

What TO Do

  • Walk to the point of claudication, rest, walk again (builds collateral circulation)
  • Daily foot hygiene and skin inspection
  • Antiplatelet therapy (aspirin, clopidogrel)
  • High-dose statins
  • Keep extremities warm
🚨 What NOT to Do in PAD
  • Do NOT elevate legs above heart (reduces blood flow to ischemic tissue)
  • Do NOT cross legs (compresses vessels)
  • Do NOT apply direct heat to limbs (burn risk — sensation impaired)
  • Avoid caffeine and tobacco (vasoconstriction)

💡 Mnemonics & Memory Tricks

Your brain remembers stories and patterns way better than facts. These memory hooks will stick with you into the exam room.

🔪 Module 1: Perioperative Care

5W

Post-Op Fever — The 5 W's

Wind (POD 1–2) = atelectasis (lungs collapsing)
Water (POD 3–5) = UTI/CAUTI
Wound (POD 3–7) = surgical site infection
Walking (POD 5+) = DVT or PE
Wonder drugs (anytime) = drug fever
MH

Malignant Hyperthermia Treatment Order

When the anesthesia fire starts in the muscles: STOP the gas → Hyperventilate with 100% O2 → Dantrolene 2.5 mg/kg IV → Cool the patient → Correct acidosis & K+. Think: "Stop. Help. Dantrolene. Cool. Correct."

PCA

PCA Safety — "Only the Patient Pushes!"

The PCA button requires conscious effort as a safety check. If the patient is sleeping they don't need pain relief. Family pressing = PCA by proxy = dangerous. Think: "Conscious = safe. Sleeping = leave alone."

💧 Module 2: Fluids & Electrolytes

💧

Hypo vs. Hypernatremia — Which Way Does the Brain Go?

HypoNa+ (too little Na+) = water rushes INTO brain cells → brain SWELLS → think "brain is bloated and confused"
HyperNa+ (too much Na+) = water pulled OUT of brain cells → brain SHRINKS → think "brain is dried out and irritable"
Mnemonic: Hypo = Brain is Bloated. Hyper = Brain is Bleached.

K+

Hyperkalemia Treatment: C-BIG-K Drop

CCalcium gluconate — stabilizes the heart membrane (works in 2 min)
BBicarb — shifts K+ into cells
IInsulin + glucose — shifts K+ into cells (check glucose after!)
GGlucose — given with insulin to prevent hypoglycemia
KKayexalate/Lokelma — removes K+ from GI tract over hours
Na

Sodium Correction Rate — "8 by 24 or ODS"

Chronic hyponatremia: correct at maximum 8–10 mEq/L per 24 hours. Go faster → you get ODS (osmotic demyelination syndrome) = irreversible brainstem damage. Think: "Slow and steady saves the myelin."
Acute hyponatremia with seizures: 3% NS to stop the seizure → then slow down.

🩸

Blood Transfusion Reaction Recognition

If fever + BACK PAIN + dark urine = acute hemolytic reaction (ABO mismatch = emergency!)
If just fever + chills (no back pain/dark urine) = febrile non-hemolytic (less dangerous)
If flushing + itching during infusion = Red Man Syndrome (rate issue, not true allergy)
If dyspnea + crackles + HTN = TACO (fluid overload from transfusion)

🧠 Module 3: Neurological

BE

BE-FAST — Stroke Recognition

BBalance — sudden loss of balance or coordination
EEyes — sudden vision changes
FFace — drooping on one side
AArm — weakness, can't raise both arms evenly
SSpeech — slurred or strange words
TTime — call 911 NOW. Note the time!
TRAP

Parkinson's Disease — 4 Cardinal Signs

TTremor — at rest, pill-rolling
RRigidity — cogwheel, lead-pipe stiffness
AAkinesia/Bradykinesia — slow movement, shuffling gait
PPostural instability — falls, can't correct balance

Think: "Parkinson's sets a TRAP for your movements."

ICP

Cushing's Triad — Late Sign of ICP (EMERGENCY!)

🚨 Hypertension (widened pulse pressure) + Bradycardia + Irregular respirations
Think: "The skull is so full, the brain is squeezing itself."
This is a PRE-HERNIATION warning. Call for help immediately and get mannitol ready!

L vs R

Left vs. Right Stroke Deficits

LEFT brain stroke = Language problems (aphasia). Patient knows what they want but can't get words out. Right body weakness. Patient is slow and cautious.

RIGHT brain stroke = Right hemisphere controls spatial awareness. Patient ignores LEFT side (neglect). Impulsive behavior. Left body weakness.

Trick: Left = Lost Language. Right = Neglects Right's Side... wait, they NEGLECT the OPPOSITE side. Right brain = Left body neglect.

❤️ Module 4: Cardiovascular

HF

Left vs. Right Heart Failure — Which Way Does the Backup Go?

Left HF = backs up into the LUNGS → WET lungs. Symptoms: crackles, dyspnea, orthopnea, pink frothy sputum.
Think: "Left = Lungs. Wet lungs mean left failure."

Right HF = backs up into the BODY → WET body. Symptoms: JVD, edema, ascites, enlarged liver.
Think: "Right = Rest of body. Puffy body means right failure."

📊

HTN Urgency vs. Emergency — The Organ Rule

Both have BP >180/120. The difference is organ damage:
⚠️ Urgency = high BP, NO organ damage → oral meds, fix over 24–48 hours
🚨 Emergency = high BP + organ damage (stroke, MI, kidney failure, pulmonary edema) → IV meds, ICU, fix MAP by 25% in 1 hour
Think: "Emergency = End-organ = Emergency department!"

ECG

ECG Lead Location — "All Indians Eat Peyote"

AAnterior — V1–V4 (LAD artery)
IInferior — II, III, aVF (RCA artery)
E(lat)Eral — I, aVL, V5–V6 (LCx artery)
PPosterior — tall R in V1–V2 (mirror image)
Dig

Digoxin Toxicity — "The Yellow Warning"

Signs: Anorexia/nausea → Visual changes (yellow-green halos) → Arrhythmias → Bradycardia
Think: "I ate too much digoxin and now everything looks YELLOW and my heart is SLOW."
Dangerous combo: Digoxin + Hypokalemia = LETHAL (low K+ doubles toxicity). Always check K+ before giving digoxin!

A vs V

Arterial vs. Venous Ulcers — "AV Difference"

Arterial: Toes, pressure points. Painful. Pale/cool skin. Pulse absent. "Three P's of Arterial!"
Venous: Medial ankle. Mild pain. Moist/weepy wound. Brown skin. "Three M's of Venous!"
Treatment: Arterial = revascularize, NO compression. Venous = compression (after checking ABI!).
